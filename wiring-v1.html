The whole idea in one sentence

The ESP32 does not talk to the garage door. It presses the button on an old remote that is already enrolled with the door — and it presses that button through a chip that passes light instead of electricity, so the two halves never touch.

The one part that isn't obvious

The PC817 optocoupler is a black 4-legged chip. Inside it there is a tiny lamp on one side and a light-operated switch on the other, sealed in the same plastic with no metal between them. The ESP32 lights the lamp; the switch closes; the switch is wired across the remote's button pads, so the remote sees exactly what it sees when your thumb presses the button.

Its legs are not interchangeable. Two are the lamp side, two are the switch side, and the switch side only conducts one way round.

The remote's side, as measured

The MITTO's board carries four surface-mount switches around the slot for its single 12 V cell. The one enrolled on the door is the upper-left switch with the remote held nose up — beside the "IMEX" print and two large solder-blob holes. It has five pads, not two, and they were mapped with the multimeter on 2026-09-03.

Battery out, button up: pads 1 and 5 beep to each other, pads 3 and 4 beep to each other, pad 2 is only the metal case. Button pressed: 1–3, 5–3 and 1–4 all beep — the two groups join. Battery in, button up, red probe on pad 4 and black on pad 5: about +12 V. So the 3/4 group is the + side of the switch.

That fixes the wiring: PC817 pin 4 → pad 4, PC817 pin 3 → pad 5. Both sit on the switch's bottom edge, apart from each other and well away from the case pad. Pads 3 and 1 are the same nets as 4 and 5 — spares if a pad ever lifts.

How it physically goes together

The schematic above shows what connects to what. This one shows what the finished thing actually looks like. There is no circuit board: the parts are soldered leg-to-leg in mid-air — "dead-bug" style — and every joint is sealed in heat-shrink. The whole assembly hangs off two Dupont jumpers whose female ends simply push onto the ESP32's header pins. Nothing is ever soldered to the ESP32: pull two connectors and the board is free for reflashing or replacement.

At the remote: two joints, and where the wires go

Everything on the ESP32 side is forgiving. The remote is not: its pads are a millimetre and a half long, they sit against a plastic switch body, and the board is fifteen years old with no replacement. The technique is preparation done in advance so each actual joint takes about one second of iron contact — and a wire route that keeps the leads off every plunger, because once the shell is back on, the rubber keypad presses down on whatever lies on top of a switch.

The two pad joints, step by step

  1. Battery out. Tape the board by its edges to a piece of cardboard, nose up, enrolled switch toward you. Good light; a magnifier or the phone camera zoomed in if you have one.
  2. Iron at about 330 °C with a fine tip, freshly cleaned and tinned. Leaded 0.8 mm rosin-core solder flows at lower heat — use it if you have it.
  3. Make the two leads. 10–15 cm each of the 26–28 AWG stranded wire, two colours — red for pad 4, dark for pad 5. That colour is the polarity marker for the rest of the build. Strip 2 mm, twist the strands, flux, tin until the strands are one solid shiny stub, then trim the stub to 1.5 mm — no longer than the pad, or it overhangs and bridges. Slide the heat-shrink for J4/J5 onto the leads now.
  4. Prepare the pads. Isopropyl on a cotton bud over the switch area, then flux on pads 4 and 5. Add no solder: the pads already carry the factory solder, and that is all a joint this size needs.
  5. Pad 5 first. Lay the stub flat on the pad, pointing straight down the board, away from the switch body. Hold the lead with tweezers, or tape it to the board 2 cm back so both hands are free. Touch the iron to the stub and the pad together: within about one second the pad's solder melts and flows up around the stub. Lift straight up, hold the lead still for two seconds. Never more than two seconds of contact — the switch terminals sit in plastic.
  6. Look at it. Good: shiny, concave, the stub sunk into the fillet. Bad: a dull grainy ball sitting on top — reflux and touch once more. Let it cool ten seconds.
  7. Pad 4 the same way, stub pointing down and to the right so the two leads diverge. Keep the stub and the iron clear of the switch's metal shell — it is the same metal as pad 2.
  8. If a pad lifts, stop. Do not rework it. Pad 3 is the same net as 4, pad 1 the same as 5: move that lead to the spare pad. Desoldering braid takes off a blob or a bridge.
  9. Clean and check, battery out. Isopropyl over both joints. Then continuity, probing only the bare lead ends and the untouched pads — never the fresh joints: red lead ↔ pad 3 beeps, dark lead ↔ pad 1 beeps (both joints conduct); red ↔ dark is silent, and beeps when the plunger is pressed; neither lead beeps to pad 2.
  10. Check, battery in. Red probe on the red lead, black on the dark lead: the same +12 V as across the pads. Press the plunger — the door must still respond, and nothing may happen without a press.
  11. Lock it down. A small blob of hot glue over each joint and the first 5 mm of lead; tape the leads to the board a few centimetres on. A tug now pulls on the glue and tape, not on the pad. Route as in the figure.

Four ways to wreck this

Read these before step 01. Each one is cheap to avoid and expensive to undo.

Order of work

Don't kill the programming key

Until the new MITTO COOL C2 is enrolled, this remote is the only key that can enroll it. Soldering leaves it working; a cooked switch or a torn pad might not. Iron contact under two seconds, and a damaged pad means moving to its spare (3 for 4, 1 for 5), not reworking it.

Not optional

Don't skip the 10 kΩ

Without the pulldown, GPIO 26 floats while the ESP32 boots. A floating pin can read as a press — meaning a power cut could open your garage door on its own when the power returns.

Directional

Pin 4 to pad 4, pin 3 to pad 5

Measured, not guessed: pad 4 is the +12 V side. PC817 pins 4 and 3 conduct one way only — wired backwards, everything looks correct and the button never presses, hours of chasing a fault that is a swapped pair of wires.

Bare metal

Nothing touches pad 2

The middle pad on the switch's top edge is its metal shell, and the shell wraps the whole body. A lead, a solder whisker or a slipped iron tip on it shorts an unknown net. Both joints are on the bottom edge; the top edge stays untouched.

Build order

  1. Enroll the new remote. MITTO COOL C2, using the old remote as the programming key. Test it on the door several times. Nothing else in this list depends on it, but the old remote must survive everything below until this is done.
  2. Range-check the old remote from the spot where the ESP32 will live. If the door doesn't respond from there, the mounting spot moves — not the plan.
  3. Open the old remote — done 2026-09-03. Board photographed, the cell is a single 12 V cylinder: buy a spare. The door's step logic was confirmed at the same time: closed → opens, open → closes, moving → stops, stopped mid-travel → reverses.
  4. Measure the pads — done 2026-09-03. Pad map in the switch figure: pin 4 → pad 4 (+12 V), pin 3 → pad 5. Repeat the one reading before soldering: red on pad 4, black on pad 5, positive. The switched current was not measured; a remote's button feeds a logic input, microamps against the PC817's 50 mA limit. Measure it if the meter has a µA range, otherwise accept.
  5. Build the small assembly — done 2026-09-01. PC817 with the 330 Ω on pin 1 and the 10 kΩ across the GPIO/GND leads, dead-bug soldered onto the pigtails and bench-verified. Still owed: a wiggle test on each joint, and sleeving the two bare GPIO-node leads.
  6. Solder the two leads to pads 5 and 4. The step list in "At the remote" above, in that order. Then the two meter checks, the hot glue, the tape.
  7. Join leads to the PC817. Red lead to pin 4 (J4), dark lead to pin 3 (J5), heat-shrink over both. Battery in, ESP32 still unplugged: the plunger works the door, nothing happens on its own.
  8. Jumper it onto the ESP32. The pigtails' female ends push straight onto the board's male header pins — GPIO 26 and GND. The board has several GND pins and they are all the same point electrically: use the one on the same side as GPIO 26 so both connectors sit on one edge. Never solder the ESP32 itself.
  9. Flash the beacon-aware production build. pio run -e v1-ble -t upload. Plain v1 watches only the classic radio, which the logger weeks proved cannot see an arrival.
  10. Power up in DRY-RUN. The firmware boots there by default and only logs. Watch it for a session before touching the mode.
  11. First real press, supervised. Start with the door closed. Stand at the door, working remote in hand, and use the manual pulse. A press is a step command: closed opens, open closes, moving stops — be ready to correct it. Door ignores the press → lengthen the pulse; double-triggers → shorten it.
  12. Box it up. ESP32 and remote in one small box, one hole for the USB cable, nothing pressing on any of the remote's four plungers.
