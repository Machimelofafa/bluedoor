The whole idea in one sentence

The ESP32 does not talk to the garage door. It presses the button on an old remote that is already enrolled with the door — and it presses that button through a chip that passes light instead of electricity, so the two halves never touch.

The one part that isn't obvious

The PC817 optocoupler is a black 4-legged chip. Inside it there is a tiny lamp on one side and a light-operated switch on the other, sealed in the same plastic with no metal between them. The ESP32 lights the lamp; the switch closes; the switch is wired across the remote's button pads, so the remote sees exactly what it sees when your thumb presses the button.

Its legs are not interchangeable. Two are the lamp side, two are the switch side, and the switch side only conducts one way round.

Before the iron gets hot: find the + pad

The remote's two button pads are not marked. The PC817's switch side only conducts one way, so you have to know which pad is which before you solder. This is a two-minute multimeter job and it is the single measurement that decides whether the finished build works.

Three ways to wreck this

Read these before step 01. Each one is cheap to avoid and expensive to undo.

Order of work

Don't open the old remote yet

The old remote is the programming key for enrolling the new one. Enroll the MITTO COOL C2 first and confirm it works on the door. Only then does the old one become a component.

Not optional

Don't skip the 10 kΩ

Without the pulldown, GPIO 26 floats while the ESP32 boots. A floating pin can read as a press — meaning a power cut could open your garage door on its own when the power returns.

Directional

Don't guess the pad polarity

PC817 pins 4 and 3 conduct one way only. Wired backwards, everything looks correct and the button never presses — hours of chasing a fault that is a swapped pair of wires.

Build order

  1. Enroll the new remote. MITTO COOL C2, using the old remote as the programming key. Test it on the door several times. Nothing gets opened before this is solid.
  2. Range-check the old remote from the spot where the ESP32 will live. If the door doesn't respond from there, the mounting spot moves — not the plan.
  3. Open the old remote. Photograph the board, note the battery type, buy a spare.
  4. Measure the pads. DC volts across them, find +, mark it. (Figure above.)
  5. Build the small assembly. PC817 with the 330 Ω on pin 1 and the 10 kΩ across the GPIO/GND leads, soldered directly onto the flying leads. Heat-shrink every joint — no perfboard needed.
  6. Jumper it into the ESP32. Male-to-female Dupont wires into the board's existing sockets. Never solder the ESP32 itself.
  7. Solder the two thin wires to the pads. Pin 4 to +, pin 3 to −. Fine tip, flux, clean the flux off afterwards with isopropyl.
  8. Power up in DRY-RUN. The firmware boots there by default and only logs. Watch it for a session before touching the mode.
  9. First real press, supervised. Stand at the door, working remote in hand, and use the manual pulse. A press is a step command — be ready to correct it.
  10. Box it up. ESP32 and remote in one small box, one hole for the USB cable, nothing pressing on the remote's own button.
